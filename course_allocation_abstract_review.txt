Notes

- Use "without real monetary transfers" rather than "without monetary transfers" if the mechanism relies on artificial budgets or bidding points.
- Friendship preferences should be presented as your extension, not as part of the classical definition of the course allocation problem.

Recommended revised abstract
The course allocation problem is a one-sided combinatorial assignment problem in which students are assigned feasible bundles of indivisible course seats subject to course capacities, without real monetary transfers. Existing course allocation mechanisms typically model preferences as purely individual and therefore do not capture the effect of friendship relations on students' course choices. We propose a friendship-aware extension of the HBS course allocation mechanism in which each student's utility combines course preferences with a directed friendship-based social term weighted by a student-specific parameter. Beyond the initial allocation, we introduce a post-allocation improvement stage that applies local-search moves based on swap, drop-add, and hybrid operations. These moves are examined under both global-welfare and personal-improvement objectives while preserving course-capacity feasibility. The framework is deterministic and reproducible, and we systematically evaluate both the initial and improved allocations using total utility, Gini-based inequality measures, and friendship-overlap statistics across experiments. The study extends classical course allocation by incorporating asymmetric social preferences and by experimentally assessing how friendship-aware utilities together with post-allocation local improvements affect allocation quality.

Alternative 1: stricter OR/MS style
The course allocation problem is a one-sided combinatorial assignment problem in which students are assigned feasible bundles of indivisible course seats under course-capacity constraints and without real monetary transfers. Standard course allocation models typically assume independent student preferences and therefore neglect the effect of friendship relations on allocation outcomes. We present a friendship-aware extension of the HBS course allocation mechanism in which each student's utility is augmented by a directed friendship term weighted by a student-specific social coefficient. We also study a post-allocation improvement phase based on local-search moves, including swap, drop-add, and hybrid operations. The moves are evaluated under both social-welfare and personal-improvement objectives while maintaining feasibility with respect to course capacities. The framework is deterministic and reproducible, and the computational study evaluates outcomes using total utility, Gini-based inequality measures, and friendship-overlap statistics. The results are intended to quantify the effect of asymmetric social preferences and local post-draft improvements on allocation quality.

Alternative 2: polished conference-submission style
Course allocation assigns students to feasible bundles of indivisible course seats under limited capacities and without real monetary transfers. In practice, however, students often care not only about which courses they receive, but also about whether they attend them with friends, a factor ignored by most existing allocation mechanisms. We address this gap through a friendship-aware extension of the HBS course allocation mechanism, where each student's utility combines course preferences with directed friendship-based utility weighted by an individual social parameter. In addition, we introduce a post-allocation improvement phase based on swap, drop-add, and hybrid local-search moves. These moves are examined under both global-welfare and personal-improvement objectives while preserving course-capacity feasibility. We evaluate the deterministic and reproducible framework using total utility, Gini-based inequality measures, and friendship-overlap statistics. The resulting study provides an experimentally grounded extension of classical course allocation that captures asymmetric social preferences and the effect of post-draft local improvements.
